# A Mini Review of 5-HT2A Receptor Agonists: From Microtubules to Altered States of Consciousness

A short literature review written for Zoo 400 on the neurobiology of classic
psychedelics — psilocybin, LSD, and DMT — as potent agonists of the 5-HT2A
receptor, tracing their effects from the molecular scale up to whole-brain
connectivity and clinical use.

## Background

Psychedelics have a long history in indigenous cultures and re-entered Western
science with Hofmann's accidental discovery of LSD-25 in the 1940s. After a
mid-century surge of research and a decades-long political freeze, the field
revived in the 1990s and now enjoys "breakthrough therapy" status from the FDA
and NIH. Most work has targeted psychiatric disorders — addiction, depression,
anxiety, OCD — but attention has increasingly turned to the underlying
mechanisms: neural plasticity, perception, and altered states of consciousness.

## What the review covers

**Cellular and molecular actions.** Recent studies probe how 5-HT2A agonists act
at the subcellular level — modulation of proteins tied to long-term potentiation
and microtubule dynamics (Dakic et al., 2017), hallucinogen-specific signaling
pathways distinguishing psychedelic from non-psychedelic agonists
(González-Maeso et al., 2007), and effects on tubulin dynamics and neurotrophin
(TrkA/TrkB) receptor activation.

**Structural and functional plasticity.** Olson's framing of psychedelics as
"psychoplastogens" — compounds that measurably increase plasticity after a single
dose — anchors this section. Work by Ly et al. shows increased dendritic
complexity, spine growth, and synaptogenesis dependent on 5-HT2A, mTOR, and TrkB
signaling, with in vivo evidence of neurogenesis in the dentate gyrus.

**Clinical applications.** Beyond psychiatry, the review surveys emerging
evidence for psychedelics as immunomodulators (via the sigma-1 receptor) and
neuroprotective agents in traumatic brain injury and ischemia, alongside
whole-brain connectivity changes measured with fMRI and EEG.

## Takeaway

Knowledge of how 5-HT2A agonists work is expanding rapidly across scales — from
genomic changes to brain-wide activity — but significant gaps remain around
long-term effects, exact molecular mechanisms, and the roles of other receptors
(sigma-1, 5-HT1A) in their therapeutic action.
