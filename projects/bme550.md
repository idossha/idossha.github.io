# BME 550 — Microfluidic Devices for a Biomimetic Alzheimer Milieu

_Final Project (BME 550), December 2022. With Parker Esswein, Vanessa Obrycki, Derick Peters, Maya Tanna, and Isaac Krause._

A microfluidic device designed to study neuronal axon networks in a biomimetic
Alzheimer's microenvironment. The device simulates the Alzheimer's milieu to allow
testing and manipulation of cholinergic neurons, generating conversance about how
healthy neurons are affected by the disease.

## Motivation

Over six million Americans have Alzheimer's disease, with no cure and low
therapeutic-intervention efficacy. The pathology — driven by abnormal, toxic
protein build-up — progressively damages cholinergic neurons and disrupts their
axonal networks. There is a knowledge gap around the mechanisms Alzheimer's uses to
create aberrant neuronal behavior, and highly mimetic models of the in vivo milieu
are needed. A microscale device is the proper size to interface with, house, and
guide neuron growth, enabling individual study and manipulation of neurons within an
Alzheimer's environment.

## Device Design

The device consists of three functional groups: circular ports that house neuron
somas, long thinning channels for unidirectional axon growth, thin diffusion lumens
for media/nutrient delivery, a main rectangular channel to mimic the Alzheimer's
milieu, and inlet/outlet ports to drive flow. The long channels taper from 100 µm
down to 20 µm to develop a pressure gradient that, with passive pumping, induces
directional axon extension into the main 1.50 mm × 1.50 mm chamber — the region
holding a high concentration of β-amyloid peptides and phosphorylated tau proteins
where the neuronal network interacts. Seven device variants were designed with
different feature dimensions to find the optimal geometry.

## Fabrication

The devices were fabricated by SU-8 photoresist photolithography on silicon wafers:
cleaning and pre-baking the wafer, spin-coating a ~120 µm SU-8 layer, exposing
through a patterned photomask at 365 nm UV, post-exposure baking, and developing the
pattern. Exposure time had to be reduced from 27 s to 25 s to avoid overexposing and
merging features. PDMS molds (10:1 Sylgard 184) were then cast from the patterned
wafers, degassed under vacuum, cured, oxygen-plasma bonded to glass, and punched to
create the final testable devices.

## Validation & Results

Validation used both an in silico COMSOL simulation and a benchtop dye-injection
test. A SolidWorks 3D model (scaled up 10× to the milli-scale for software
constraints) was imported into COMSOL, where stationary and time-dependent laminar
flow and diluted-species-transport studies were run.

![COMSOL flow simulation](/assets/projects/BME550/BME550_COMSOL.png)

![Simulation animation](/assets/projects/BME550/BME550.gif)

The simulations showed that injecting fluid at rates from 0.0025 to 0.025 m/s
generated a uniform flow covering the entire device, including the neuron reservoir
ports, and that pressure was highest near the inlets and dropped toward the
outlets — a gradient that can be tuned to tailor factor concentrations (e.g. NGF)
for axonal growth. Benchtop testing with colored food dye and a syringe/needle
system, imaged on a Leica MZ95 stereomicroscope, was limited by fabrication issues
(channel merging, low resolution in the smaller designs, PDMS rupture), so testing
focused on the larger devices.

## Outlook

Due to fabrication difficulties the benchtop results were limited, but the COMSOL
simulations validated the expected flow behavior. Future work should include neuron
testing with functional recording methods — microelectrode arrays and external
electrodes — to determine whether the device recapitulates the 3D structure of the
Alzheimer's physiological environment and enables high-efficacy investigations of
the milieu.
